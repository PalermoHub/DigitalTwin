Hello, and welcome to the Palermo Digital Twin.
A map with the city's open data:
land registry, zoning plan, monuments, transport and much more, all in one place.
In this video we look at four things.
First:
how to use the map, from searching for a place to the card with all its data.
Second:
the plugin that brings the national data catalogue onto the map.
Third:
Geoimage, to overlay historical maps on today's Palermo.
Fourth:
where to find everything, and how to help.
Everything starts from the Guide, in the top menu.
It has 19 steps:
we actually do them, one at a time, on the map.
This is the initial view:
the centre of Palermo.
At the top, the logo and the menu with the guides.
On the left, the layers bar.
At the bottom, the search.
And in the footer, a notice I'll mention up front:
land registry, zoning plan and constraints are informational data, with no legal value.
On the phone the controls change.
At the bottom there are four tabs:
Map, Layers, Add and Menu.
Layers opens base maps, layers and filters.
Add brings together your layers, the national catalogue and Geoimage.
Menu opens the guides.
To learn about a place, tap the map and the card slides up from the bottom.
The data comes from public bodies and open data projects:
the Municipality of Palermo, the public transport company,
the land registry, the zoning plan, the census and OpenStreetMap.
The list is in Sources and notices:
for each source you'll find the date, the link to the original data and the licence.
At the top are the notices:
land registry, zoning plan and constraints have no legal value.
Layers are the themes that overlay the map.
Open the Layers tab:
the groups are in alphabetical order.
Open Territory and switch on the land registry with the checkbox.
It appears at the top as a label, and the legend at the bottom explains its colours.
You can switch on more than one and compare them:
for example buildings and land registry.
With several layers on, the orange Layer order on map card lists them all.
At the top means above, on the map.
You move them with the arrows or by dragging, and the order is remembered.
The Base map tab changes the map beneath the layers:
streets, aerial, topographic.
At the bottom are the historical maps:
15 maps of Palermo, from 1580 to 1993.
The coloured dot tells you how well the map overlaps with today's city.
Here, Palermo in 1891.
In the My layers tab you add your own data:
a file from your computer, a web address, or a map service.
It works like the QGIS Browser.
Services stay saved in your browser.
The palette button opens the colour panel.
With Colour by attribute you choose a data field and the map is coloured accordingly.
Here, buildings by population density.
The colour ramp is chosen from a list with a preview,
including Fabio Crameri's scientific scales, readable even with colour blindness.
To learn about a place, just click on the map.
The point is highlighted and the place card opens.
Highlighted areas have a legend with a colour for each entry:
a click on an entry leaves only that one on the map.
Hovering with the mouse shows a tooltip with the layer name.
And this is the strength of the map:
a single click queries land registry, zoning constraints,
road safety, cultural heritage and public transport together.
The card lists everything that overlaps at that point.
The card is divided into sections, which you choose from the tabs at the top.
Place summarises address, neighbourhood, monuments, accident risk and nearby stops.
Planning tools starts from the building, then shows the parcel,
with sheet and number, and the link to the cadastral extract.
Every section states its source.
The Monuments layer shows churches, palaces, fountains and theatres.
A click on the Teatro Massimo opens its card:
photo, description, category,
and the link to the Palermo Municipality Tourism Portal, where the texts come from.
The Municipal offices layer shows where the Municipality's offices are, grouped by location.
The card for Palazzo Palagonia lists areas and offices,
with managers and contacts, when published.
The PAI Plan group reports the Hydrogeological Plan of the Sicilian Region:
hydraulic and geomorphological hazard and risk.
A click on an area opens the card, with the PAI constraints box and the most severe class.
This is informational data:
for legal use, the official cartography prevails.
The Fires layer shows the areas burned since 2007, year by year:
the colour indicates the year.
The card reports date, location and burned areas.
Heat islands show the surface temperature in summer,
derived from Landsat satellites, for each census section.
In the legend you choose the classification method and the number of classes,
and see the trend from 2019 to 2025.
Note:
it is the surface temperature, not the air temperature.
To search, type in the field at the bottom a street,
a house number, a neighbourhood or a parcel.
The Filters tab limits the search to a district, a neighbourhood or a zone,
or lets you find a parcel by sheet and number.
For example:
choose a district and type Maqueda.
A click on the result takes the map to the spot.
The tools at the top right.
The house returns to the initial view.
The 3D button tilts the map.
Then full screen and the moon, for the dark theme.
The printer prepares a sheet with the map, legends and sources:
choose format, from A4 to A0, orientation and scale.
One last warning, an important one.
Land registry, zoning and constraints are for information only:
they do not replace the urban planning certificate or the official cadastral extracts.
Census data are estimates.
For legal use, always contact the competent offices.
Let's move on to the national catalogue.
First, a couple of sentences on what it is.
The RNDT, the National Register of Spatial Data,
is the official Italian catalogue of geographic data:
it collects the records of thousands of maps published by municipalities,
regions, ministries and other bodies.
It's like a library:
it tells you what exists, who produced it and where to consult it.
Inside this map, the catalogue is the openrndt-geolibre plugin,
conceived and written by Andrea Borruso of onData.
Without his work this feature would not exist:
it was adapted to Palermo with very few changes,
thanks to its excellent architecture.
You find it in the toolbar at the top right:
it's the icon with the two layers.
The panel opens on the right side,
and the Card and RNDT tabs let you switch between them.
With Esc you close it.
The plugin interface is in English:
it's only a few words, don't be put off.
Type what you're looking for, for example protected areas, and press Search.
The search is always limited to the Palermo area.
You can filter by type, data or services, and by format:
WMS, WFS or ArcGIS REST.
In the advanced filters there are theme, body and dates.
Choose a result:
the card opens, with title, description, responsible body and available services.
Press Open on the service and tick the layer you're interested in:
here, the Regional reserves.
WMS services are added as images.
With Add features, and with WFS services,
you bring onto the map the actual features, with their information.
Here are the reserves on the map.
You find them among the added layers and in the RNDT group of the layers bar:
with the eye you switch them on and off, with the bin you remove them.
They remember themselves:
reopen the map and they are still there.
A click on the map also queries these layers.
The answers arrive in the Other RNDT data tab of the card,
with a box for each layer.
Here, for example, Monte Pellegrino, with type and manager.
You have to click right on the feature.
Two things to know.
The WFS download has a cap of 10,000 objects:
for very dense layers, like cadastral parcels, WMS is better.
And if a service doesn't respond,
it stays in the list as unavailable, without being deleted.
And your files?
They are loaded from My layers:
GeoJSON, KML, GPX, zipped Shapefile, CSV with latitude and longitude.
Of each file only the features inside the Municipality of Palermo are kept.
In short:
search, choose, add, click.
Four steps to bring onto the map the official data about Palermo.
Last feature:
Geoimage.
It lets you overlay a historical map, or any image,
on modern cartography, and georeference it:
that is, give it real geographic coordinates, using control points, the GCPs.
First step:
frame the area with the search, for example the Teatro Massimo,
so the image loads already close to the right place.
Drag the file into the Upload historical map box, or click it.
Here, a plan of Palermo from 1891:
it appears right away in the centre of the map, with the positioning handles.
With the handles you move it, rotate it and resize it.
The corners have two modes, scale and distort,
useful for maps that are not rectangular.
Now the control points.
Press Add GCP.
For each point you need two clicks:
the first on a recognisable place in the historical image,
a crossroads, a building, a monument.
The second on the same place in the modern map.
You need at least three, ideally at the corners.
With three points you can press Align image to GCPs:
the app calculates the transformation and moves the image to the right place.
Check the error.
The RMSE is the mean error in metres:
the lower, the better.
In the table each point has its residual:
green if it's normal, red if it's much higher.
If it's high, move the less precise points or add more.
To check, use the Swipe:
a line divides the map, on the left the historical map, on the right today's base.
Or the Spotlight:
a circle that follows the mouse reveals the base beneath the image.
When you're satisfied, export:
KMZ for Google Earth and QGIS, GeoTIFF with embedded coordinates,
the points for the QGIS georeferencer, the world file, or the project in JSON.
The project saves itself in the browser.
And if you want more accuracy, we recommend MapWarper.
Let's sum up in three points.
One:
a click tells you everything about a place, with the source.
Two:
the national catalogue brings official data onto the map.
Three:
Geoimage compares today's Palermo with yesterday's.
